{\large\bfseries Why accurate neural predictions do not guarantee effective neural control\par}

\textbf{Summary.}
Encoding models of visual cortex can predict responses to natural images yet fail to generate images that control those responses. We develop a regression theory motivated by a closed-loop comparison of ten models at 25 recording sites in five macaques. The key is a change of evaluation distribution: prediction tests a model on natural images, i.i.d. as training images, whereas control tests it along its own optimized directions. Low-variance image directions can hide estimation errors that dominate gradient-based synthesis. Random matrix theory shows how finite data and noisy responses inflate a fitted model's predicted gain without increasing its alignment with the neuron, and why prediction-selected regularization need not prevent this failure. For fixed feature maps, we construct equally predictive models with sharply different control and isolate an additional gradient-geometry factor. Extended to nonlinear features, this prognostic factor is correlated with biological control, whereas held-out prediction error is largely uninformative. The same account explains how poor generators can recognize effective stimuli produced by other models. Neural encoding models should therefore be evaluated on their gradients or generated stimuli when used for control.

\head{The experimental puzzle.}
Prince, Wang, et al.~[1] recorded naturalistic-image responses at 25 sites in five macaques, spanning V3/V4, inferotemporal cortex (IT) and superior temporal sulcus (STS). For each of ten fixed deep-network backbones $\phi_m$, cross-validated ridge regression fitted a linear readout, $f_m(x)=\hat w_m^\top\phi_m(x)$. Feature accentuation~[2] then modified each seed image using regularized gradient ascent or descent on $f_m$ to reach graded response targets within and beyond the neuron's natural-image range (Fig.~\ref{fig:experiment}A). Presenting these images to the same sites tested whether measured responses matched the model's targets. Despite similar natural-image predictivity, trained models differed sharply in control; an accurate predictor could barely modulate its target site (Fig.~\ref{fig:experiment}B,C). Unsuccessful controllers could still predict responses to another model's effective stimuli. We ask how this fit--optimize--test workflow turns a good predictor into a bad controller.

\head{From experiment to a solvable model.}
We preserve the backbone--readout structure but replace $\phi(x)$ by fixed linear features $Fx$. Natural inputs $x\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma)$ drive a noisy linear neuron, $y=x^\top\bt+\sigma\epsilon$, with independent unit-variance noise $\epsilon$. Ridge regression on $n$ examples fits $f(x)=\hat w^\top Fx=x^\top\bh$, with pixel gradient $\bh=F^\top\hat w$. We retain ridge fitting but idealize regularized synthesis as the straight path $x=x_0+\alpha\bh$. From a centered seed $x_0=0$, mean neural and predicted response changes are $\alpha N$ and $\alpha D$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{slope}_{\rm control}=\frac ND=
\frac{\bh^\top\bt}{\|\bh\|^2}\,
\begin{subarray}{l}
\text{actual neural gain}\\[8pt]
\text{model-predicted gain}
\end{subarray},
\qquad R^2_{\rm control}=1-\left(\frac DN-1\right)^2.
\label{eq:geometry}
\end{equation}
Alignment $N$ is the actual neural gain; squared norm $D$ is the model's claimed gain. For $N>0$, $D<N$ means underprediction (slope $>1$), $D=N$ means perfect calibration, and $D>N$ means overprediction (slope $<1$), which could lead to negative $R^2$ (when $D>2N$). Natural-image prediction instead measures $E_{\rm gen}=(\bh-\bt)^\top\Sigma(\bh-\bt)$, downweighting low-variance directions. Errors along these directions can therefore inflate the model's claimed gain $D$ with little predictive cost or corresponding neural gain $N$.

\head{Why cross-validation does not remove the problem.}
The failure already occurs for $F=I$, with no backbone geometry. Random matrix theory~[3] gives leading approximations as dimension and sample size grow proportionally. The mean weight is the teacher shrunk along each covariance eigenvector:
\begin{equation}
\mb:=\Sigma(\Sigma+\kappa I)^{-1}\bt
=\sum_k\frac{s_k}{s_k+\kappa}\,b_k u_k,
\qquad \Sigma u_k=s_k u_k,\quad b_k=u_k^\top\bt.
\label{eq:mean-shrinkage}
\end{equation}
\begin{samepage}
Here $\kappa$ is the effective ridge penalty accounting for finite sampling; each shrinkage factor $s_k/(s_k+\kappa)\in[0,1]$.
Finite data and response noise add fluctuations to fitted weight, inflating the squared norm by $V$ without changing the leading alignment $N$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{slope}_{\rm control}\simeq
\frac{\mb^\top\bt}{\|\mb\|^2+V},
\qquad
V=\frac{E_{\rm gen}+\sigma^2}{n}
\sum_k\frac{s_k}{(s_k+\kappa)^2},
\label{eq:inflation}
\end{equation}\par
\end{samepage}
\begin{samepage}
For the mean alone, $\|\mb\|^2\le\mb^\top\bt$: the student underpredicts. Inflation increases the model-predicted gain $D$ without increasing the neural gain $N$. It first compensates for shrinkage, giving $D=N$, then causes overprediction once $D>N$.\par
\end{samepage}
\begin{samepage}
Prediction-optimal and control-calibrated penalties satisfy different spectral conditions for anisotropic inputs and a structured teacher. Natural-image simulations confirm that cross-validation preserves good prediction while control deteriorates (Fig.~\ref{fig:evidence}A).\par
\end{samepage}

\head{Why equally predictive feature maps control differently.}
Returning to general $F$ explains why equally predictive backbones control differently. Prediction depends on the feature covariance $F\Sigma F^\top$ and its relationship to the teacher; control also depends on how features map back into pixels $FF^\top$. We construct transformations preserving the joint feature--response distribution, hence the predicted generalization curve and its optimal penalty stay invariant, while control changes (Fig.~\ref{fig:evidence}B). Increasing input anisotropy can make this dissociation arbitrarily severe.

The feature-gradient geometry enters the norm inflation, 
\begin{equation}
V_F=\frac{E_{\rm gen}+\sigma^2}{n}\,T_F(\kappa),
\qquad
T_F(\kappa)=\sum_{k:s_k>0}
\left(\frac{s_k}{s_k+\kappa}\right)^2
\frac{\|g_k\|^2}{g_k^\top\Sigma g_k},
\label{eq:features}
\end{equation}
where $(s_k,v_k)$ now denote feature-covariance eigenpairs and $g_k=F^\top v_k$ is a feature direction's pixel gradient. The sum weights features that survive regularization and compares their gradient energy with natural-image variation along that gradient. Thus, control is fragile when well-estimated features send their gradients into weakly constrained pixel directions, making $T_F$ large without changing prediction error.

\head{From linear theory to biological control.}
For nonlinear features $\phi$, we replace $F$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:features} by the Jacobian of mapping $J_\phi$ at seed images, i.e. $T_\phi=\sum_k s_k/(s_k+\kappa)^2\|J_\phi(x)^\top v_k\|^2$. %we retain the spectral weights $s_k/(s_k+\kappa)^2$ and measure squared pixel-gradient norms $\|J_\phi(x)^\top v_k\|^2$. 
% their weighted sum, averaged over ten seeds, gives $T_\phi$, 
This diagnostic can be computed from the fitted model before a control experiment. Across nine trained models at 25 sites (225 site--model pairs), $\log_{10}T_\phi$ correlates with site-centered control slope at $r=-0.61$, compared with $r=0.02$ for log held-out mean squared error (Fig.~\ref{fig:evidence}C). This supports our heuristic nonlinear extension as a prognostic predictor of control success. %This association weakens substantially without the two adversarially trained models. It supports the diagnostic descriptively; the nonlinear extension is not a proved control-error formula.

\head{Recognition without generation.}
For independent pixel-space ridge fits at the same penalty, fluctuations cancel in the peer weight cross-product, removing the leading self-norm inflation: $D_{\rm peer}\simeq\|\mb\|^2\le\mb^\top\bt\simeq N_{\rm peer}$. Thus peer evaluation underpredicts (leading slope $\ge1$, $R^2\ge0$), while self-evaluation can overpredict with negative $R^2$. Reanalysis of neural data from~[1] supports this contrast: 80 of 90 peer slopes exceed one; median peer slope and $R^2$ are $1.35$ and $0.04$, versus $0.38$ and $-4.17$ for self-evaluation.
